# Tool-Augmented Factuality Checking

FacTool (Chern et al., arXiv 2307.13528, 2023) replaces asking a model whether an answer is
true with a pipeline that gathers outside evidence for each claim before judging it.

## The pipeline

1. **Claim extraction.** Split the response into atomic, self-contained claims of under about
   15 words each.
2. **Query generation.** Write a query per claim: a web search for facts, a test case or
   snippet for code, a Python expression for arithmetic, a scholar search for citations.
3. **Tool querying.** Run the queries (web search, a Python interpreter, Google Scholar).
4. **Evidence collection.** Keep the returned snippets or execution results.
5. **Agreement verification.** A model decides, claim by claim, whether the evidence supports
   the claim, and the response is scored from the claims.

## Results with GPT-4 as verifier (self-reported)

Response-level F1, with "factual" as the positive class. All responses were generated by
ChatGPT; the baseline is GPT-4 checking the response itself with a 3-shot chain-of-thought
prompt and no tools.

| Domain (responses, factual : non-factual) | FacTool F1 | Self-check F1 |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge QA (50, 23 : 27) | 71.8 | 57.1 |
| Code, HumanEval (164, 109 : 55) | 92.1 | 85.3 |
| Math, GSM-Hard (100, 47 : 53) | 80.4 | 70.6 |
| Scientific citations (100, 10 : 90) | 94.7 | 21.5 |

The paper reports that the self-check labels claims "True" considerably more often than FacTool
does; its precision is lower in every domain (12.7 vs 100 on citations).

## Caveats

- **The verifier must be strong.** With ChatGPT as verifier on knowledge QA (233 claims),
  zero-shot self-checking beat FacTool on claim-level F1, 84.9 vs 81.3; the authors put it down
  to reasoning errors.
- **Checking arithmetic is not checking reasoning.** In math, claim-level F1 was 99.0 but
  response-level F1 only 80.4, because only the calculations are verified, not whether the
  steps lead to the answer.
- **A different check from RAG faithfulness.** The evidence is the open web, not against a
  supplied context. Being faithful to a context and being correct about the world are
  different checks ([[Grounding Claim Methodology]]).

## Limits

The samples are small (50 knowledge-QA responses, only 10 factual citation responses) and no
confidence intervals or significance tests are given. The authors labelled the data
themselves, with no inter-annotator agreement. Claim extraction is scored only on summaries
from another dataset, not on the responses verified. No cost, latency or call counts are
reported, and the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 versions used are 2023 snapshots.

## Definitions from two 2023 surveys

Wang et al. (arXiv 2310.07521) define factuality as generating content consistent with facts that can be traced to reliable sources for instance dictionaries, Wikipedia and textbooks. They treat it as a separate axis from hallucination, which they define as departure from the source or prompt. Their table gives four cells: an output can be unfaithful to the prompt yet factually correct, and a non-factual output need not be a hallucination (for example "I don't know" or a partly correct answer). They report, second-hand, that GPT-3.5 as a grader of answer correctness on NQ and TriviaQA gave negative results even with the gold answer shown. The survey runs no experiments of its own, excludes summarisation faithfulness, and warns that its method-comparison rows are not directly comparable. Rawte et al. (arXiv 2309.05922) define hallucination as content not based on factual or accurate information, with no faithfulness-versus-factuality split. That survey also reports no results of its own; it groups about 25 papers by modality, its coverage ends September 2023, and the extraction notes citation errors in it, so neither survey is a source for the numbers it restates.
