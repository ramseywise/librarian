# Behavioral probe bank

STAR questions grouped by competency, with the probes an interviewer uses to dig in.

Source: [ai-engineering-gym](https://github.com/alexeygrigorev/ai-engineering-gym), converted 2026-10-29. Coaching material only —
the gym's sample answers are someone else's career and are deliberately not carried over.
Every answer you rehearse must trace to `jobs/materials/evidence/evidence-store.md`.

## ambiguity

### How do you lead under risk and uncertainty? Tell me about a time you did

`lead-under-uncertainty` · hard

**Probes** — the follow-ups to expect

- What made the situation uncertain: unknown data, new tech, shifting requirements?
- How did you decide when you had enough information to act?
- How did you keep the team moving without false certainty?
- What was your plan if your bet was wrong?
- How did you communicate the risk upward and to the team?
- How did it turn out, and what did you learn about deciding under uncertainty?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation is genuinely ambiguous: unclear requirements, unproven approach, or incomplete data (common in AI/ML work)
- Task shows you were responsible for moving forward despite the fog
- Action centers on YOUR process: framing assumptions, de-risking with small experiments, making reversible bets, communicating uncertainty honestly
- Shows decisiveness balanced with adaptability: not paralysis, not recklessness
- Result: progress or a good outcome despite the risk, and how you course-corrected
- Reflection on a repeatable approach to leading under uncertainty

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Name the specific unknowns: vague 'it was uncertain' undersells the challenge.
- Show a method: de-risk cheaply, make reversible bets, set checkpoints, communicate honestly.
- Highlight a course-correction: leading under uncertainty means adapting as data arrives.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer skeleton</summary>

```text
# STAR SKELETON — NOT a real personal story. Fill with your own.
S (Situation): A project with major unknowns (e.g. whether an LLM approach would
   even meet the quality bar, or fast-moving requirements).
T (Task): What you owned: keep the team progressing and make good calls.
A (Action): The concrete steps YOU took: named the key uncertainties, ran cheap
   experiments to reduce them, made reversible decisions, set checkpoints,
   communicated risk transparently.
R (Result): The outcome and how you adapted as you learned, plus a one-line
   lesson on deciding under uncertainty.
```

</details>

### Describe a time you had to quickly learn a new technology or methodology

`learn-new-technology-quickly` · easy

**Probes** — the follow-ups to expect

- Why did you have to learn it fast: new project, a gap on the team?
- How did you approach learning it efficiently?
- How did you get productive before you were an expert?
- Where did you get stuck and how did you get unstuck?
- How well did the result hold up, and would experts agree with your choices?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation names a specific new technology/methodology and why speed mattered (highly relevant in fast-moving AI)
- Task shows what you needed to deliver with it
- Action reveals YOUR learning strategy: docs, prototypes, experts, small experiments, learning by shipping
- Shows you became productive quickly without faking expertise
- Result: delivered with the new tech, quantified, and the skill stuck
- Reflection on how you approach ramping on anything new

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- This maps to adaptability, so foreground your learning method, which transfers to anything.
- Show 'learning by doing': a prototype or spike beats 'I read the docs'.
- Prove you got real results and can name your repeatable ramp-up approach.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer skeleton</summary>

```text
# STAR SKELETON — NOT a real personal story. Fill with your own.
S (Situation): A project needed a technology you didn't yet know (e.g. a new
   framework, a vector DB, an agent orchestration library) on a short timeline.
T (Task): What you owned: ship using it despite the learning curve.
A (Action): The concrete steps YOU took: found the fastest reliable sources,
   built a small prototype, relied on experts/community, validated as you went.
R (Result): Delivered with the new tech, quantified, plus a one-line lesson on
   how you ramp fast.
```

</details>

### What would you do if, midway through a project, you realized it was unfeasible? Tell me about a time this happened

`project-unfeasible-midway` · hard

**Probes** — the follow-ups to expect

- How did you conclude it was actually unfeasible versus just hard?
- What evidence did you gather before making that call?
- How did you deliver that news, and to whom?
- What alternatives did you propose?
- How did you avoid sunk-cost thinking?
- What was salvaged, and what did you learn about killing or pivoting projects?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation is a project that hit a real feasibility wall (data unavailable, quality bar unreachable, cost prohibitive)
- Shows rigor in confirming infeasibility rather than giving up early
- Action centers on YOUR judgment: gathering evidence, raising it, proposing a pivot or graceful stop
- Demonstrates courage and stakeholder communication: you didn't hide the bad news
- Result: value salvaged (pivot, learnings, resources redirected), not just 'we cancelled it'
- Reflection on avoiding sunk cost and validating feasibility earlier

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Prove the wall was real: show the evidence, not just a gut feeling.
- Show the courage to raise it early and propose a path forward, not just stop.
- Salvage something (learnings, a pivot) so the story ends on judgment, not defeat.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer skeleton</summary>

```text
# STAR SKELETON — NOT a real personal story. Fill with your own.
S (Situation): A project that, partway in, showed it couldn't meet its goal (e.g.
   the model couldn't reach the required accuracy, or the data didn't exist).
T (Task): What you owned: make the right call and protect the org's time.
A (Action): The concrete steps YOU took: validated the wall with evidence,
   surfaced it early, proposed alternatives (pivot, reduced scope, or stop),
   redirected the effort.
R (Result): What was salvaged and the outcome, plus a one-line lesson on feasibility
   checks and avoiding sunk-cost traps.
```

</details>

## conflict

### Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a teammate

`conflict-with-teammate` · medium

**Probes** — the follow-ups to expect

- What did you do differently from the other person, specifically?
- How did you decide when to raise it versus let it go?
- Whom did you talk to first, and why?
- Did you involve your manager, and why or why not?
- How did you keep it professional and avoid it escalating?
- What was the measurable outcome, and what did you learn?
- Looking back, what would you do differently?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation is concrete: a real, specific disagreement with named context (project, deadline, decision)
- Focuses on YOUR specific actions ('I proposed…', 'I asked…'), not what the team did
- Shows conflict-resolution skills: listening to the other view, communicating your position, seeking common ground
- Stays professional, doesn't blame or disparage the teammate
- Result is positive and quantified where possible (shipped on time, hours saved, relationship preserved)
- Ends with a reflection: what you learned or would do differently

<details><summary>Answer skeleton</summary>

```text
# Optional model STRUCTURE only — NOT a real personal story. Fill with your own.
S (Situation): Set the scene: the project, the two of you, and the specific
   technical or process disagreement (e.g. two approaches to hitting a deadline).
T (Task): What you owned and what was at stake (shipping on time, quality bar).
A (Action): The concrete steps YOU took: heard the other view, laid out
   trade-offs, proposed a structured way to decide (e.g. a short review), reached
   a shared decision without escalating.
R (Result): The outcome, quantified if you can (delivered on time, X hours saved,
   relationship intact) plus one sentence on what you learned.
```

</details>

### Tell me about a time you convinced someone to change their mind

`convinced-someone-change-mind` · medium

- What evidence or reasoning did you bring?
- How did you understand their position before pushing yours?
- Was there a moment they started to shift, and what caused it?

- What was the result, and did the relationship stay intact?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation is a concrete decision where you disagreed with someone (peer, lead, manager)
- Shows you understood their view first: persuasion by listening, not steamrolling
- Action centers on YOUR approach: data, a prototype, a small experiment, reframing the trade-off
- Demonstrates intellectual honesty: you were open to being wrong yourself
- Result: the mind changed AND the decision was better, ideally quantified, and the relationship preserved
- Reflection on influencing without authority

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Show you earned the right to persuade by understanding their view first.
- Show the pivot as a prototype, a benchmark, a number that shifted the room.
- Signal you'd have committed to their call if the data went the other way.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer skeleton</summary>


### Tell me about a time you handled a difficult stakeholder

`difficult-stakeholder` · medium

- What did you do specifically to rebuild trust or realign?
- Did you push back on anything, and how did you frame it?

- What was the outcome, and what would you do differently?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation names a concrete stakeholder and the specific friction (scope creep, shifting priorities, distrust of the AI approach)
- Shows you sought to understand their underlying interest, not just their stated demand
- Focuses on YOUR actions: how you communicated, set expectations, or negotiated trade-offs
- Stays professional and empathetic, with no blaming the stakeholder
- Result shows realignment or delivery, quantified where possible (scope agreed, launch met, NPS/trust restored)
- Ends with a reflection on managing stakeholders proactively next time

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Open by making the stakeholder sympathetic, not a villain: what pressure were they under?
- Spend most of your airtime on Action: the specific conversations and how you reframed the problem.
- Land a measurable Result and a one-line lesson about managing expectations earlier.

</details>

## failure

### Describe a project that didn't go as planned or where your AI solution failed

`ai-solution-failed` · medium

**Probes** — the follow-ups to expect

- What was the AI solution supposed to do, and how did you define success?
- How did the failure show up: offline metrics, production, or user feedback?
- What was the root cause: data, evaluation gap, distribution shift, or wrong problem framing?
- How did you catch it, and how quickly?
- What did you do to recover or contain the impact?
- What changed in how you build and evaluate AI systems afterward?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation describes a concrete AI/ML solution and its intended goal
- Names the failure honestly and how it surfaced (metric, incident, user harm)
- Action shows AI-specific diagnosis: data issues, eval blind spots, train/serve skew, hallucination, distribution shift
- Takes ownership and shows containment/recovery, not just a post-mortem
- Result: what improved and the durable change (better eval, monitoring, guardrails, tighter problem framing)
- Reflection shows AI maturity, treating failures as evaluation and process gaps

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Show AI-specific depth: point at data, evaluation, or drift, not just 'a bug'.
- Own the miss, then focus on the diagnosis and the systemic fix (better eval/monitoring).
- Frame the lesson as improved rigor, which is exactly what AI interviewers probe for.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer skeleton</summary>

```text
# STAR SKELETON — NOT a real personal story. Fill with your own.
S (Situation): An AI feature/model with a clear goal (e.g. a classifier, a RAG
   assistant, a recommendation) that underperformed in production.
T (Task): What you owned and how success was defined.
A (Action): The concrete steps YOU took: diagnosed the root cause (data, eval,
   drift, framing), contained user impact, and fixed the underlying gap.
R (Result): The recovery and the durable improvement to your eval/monitoring/
   guardrails, plus a one-line lesson on building reliable AI.
```

</details>

### What's a mistake you made, and what did you learn from it?

`mistake-and-learning` · easy

**Probes** — the follow-ups to expect

- Why did the mistake happen, and what did you miss?
- What was the impact, and who else was affected?
- How did you handle it once you realized?
- What specifically did you change afterward?
- Has that change actually prevented a repeat since?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation is a genuine, non-trivial mistake with real stakes (not a humblebrag or a fake weakness)
- Takes full ownership in the first person
- Action shows how you responded: owned it, fixed it, communicated honestly
- The lesson is specific and led to a concrete change in behavior or process
- Shows growth, that the change stuck, and an honest account of impact
- Tone is self-aware and constructive, not defensive or overly self-critical

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Choose a real mistake with stakes, because a disguised strength reads as evasive.
- Spend little time on the error and most on ownership, the fix, and the lesson.
- Make the lesson a concrete process change you can show actually stuck.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer skeleton</summary>

```text
# STAR SKELETON — NOT a real personal story. Fill with your own.
S (Situation): A real mistake you made (e.g. shipping without a guardrail, making
   an assumption that broke something, mis-scoping an estimate).
T (Task): What you were responsible for.
A (Action): The concrete steps YOU took after realizing: owned it, fixed the
   immediate damage, communicated, then changed the underlying cause.
R (Result): How it resolved and the durable change you made, plus what you learned.
```

</details>

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Pick a real misjudgment with genuine stakes: a trivial one signals you're hiding.

- End on a concrete, durable change to your process, not a vague 'I learned to be careful'.

</details>

### Tell me about a time you had to explain a complex technical concept to someone without a technical background

`explain-technical-concept` · easy

**Probes** — the follow-ups to expect

- Who was the audience and why did they need to understand it?
- What analogy or framing did you use to make it land?
- How did you check that they actually understood?
- What did you deliberately leave out, and why?
- How did explaining it change the decision they made?

**What a strong answer does**

- Situation names a concrete non-technical audience (exec, sales, customer, legal) and a real decision at stake
- Shows empathy for what the audience needed and already knew
- Action centers on YOUR communication choices: analogy, visual, plain language, dropping jargon
- Demonstrates you verified understanding, not just talked at them
- Result ties the explanation to a decision or outcome (buy-in secured, risk understood, project unblocked)
- Reflection on how clear communication drove impact

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

- Name the audience and the decision first, which frames why clarity mattered.
- In Action, quote the actual analogy or framing you used. Specifics beat 'I explained it simply'.
- Close with the decision your explanation unblocked, not just 'they understood'.
